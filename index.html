The Detour

When the Road Doesn't Go Where You Planned

Have you ever felt certain you were headed in the right direction — only to find the road leading somewhere else entirely?

It was a Tuesday morning when a gentleman climbed into the back of my taxi and gave me an address I knew well. Simple run, I thought. Twenty minutes there, back before lunch. I'd driven that road a thousand times. But two miles out, the road was flooded — an overnight rain had washed everything sideways.

I took the long way around. My passenger, a quiet man in a pressed shirt, had been staring at his phone the whole ride. When we finally pulled up, he looked out the window and said, "This isn't where I asked to go." And I told him, "No sir. But it's where you needed to be." His daughter lived just around the corner. He hadn't seen her in seven years.

I don't know how God arranges things. I just know He does. That detour wasn't mine to plan. I was just the driver. But somewhere between the flooded road and the long way around, God was working a reunion that no direct route could have managed.

We think detours are delays. God calls them directions.

Closing Prayer

Lord, when the road floods and I must take the long way around — remind me that You have never once lost the map. My detours are not Your dead ends. Lead me where I need to be, not just where I planned to go. Amen.

Straight from Bimini

Max's Chicken Souse

In Bahamian culture, chicken souse is more than a meal — it's a healing. Made on Saturday mornings, shared after late nights, served at the table of anyone who needs it. This recipe has been in Max's family as long as the road itself.

  • Whole chicken, cut into pieces3 lbs
  • Lime juice, freshly squeezed3 limes
  • White potatoes, cubed2 large
  • White onion, sliced thin1 large
  • Bird pepper (Bahamian hot pepper)1–2, whole
  • Bay leaves3 leaves
  • Fresh thyme4 sprigs
  • Allspice berries6 whole
  • Salt & black pepperto taste
  • Water8 cups
  • Celery, sliced2 stalks
  1. Wash chicken pieces well with lime juice and cold water. Let sit 10 minutes, then rinse again.
  2. Bring 8 cups of water to a boil in a large pot. Add chicken, bay leaves, allspice, and thyme.
  3. Reduce heat to medium. Skim any foam from the surface as the broth builds — this is what clears the broth.
  4. After 20 minutes, add onion, celery, and potatoes. Add the whole bird pepper but do not break it — the heat should be felt, not overwhelming.
  5. Season with salt and pepper. Cook another 25–30 minutes until potatoes are tender and chicken is falling from the bone.
  6. Taste and adjust seasoning. Squeeze in fresh lime juice just before serving. Serve hot with Johnny Bread.
  • All-purpose flour2 cups
  • Baking powder1 tbsp
  • Salt½ tsp
  • Sugar1 tbsp
  • Cold butter, cubed2 tbsp
  • Whole milk¾ cup
Mix dry ingredients. Cut in cold butter until crumbly. Add milk and stir until just combined — do not overwork. Pat into a round about ¾-inch thick. Cook on a lightly oiled griddle over medium heat, 6–8 minutes per side, until golden. Tear by hand and serve alongside the souse.
A Bowl of Grace on a Saturday Morning

In Bimini, they say you can tell how someone was raised by how they make their souse. Whether they use chicken or sheep tongue, whether the broth runs clear or cloudy, whether they add too much pepper or just enough — it all tells a story.

Max's souse is his mother's recipe, adjusted over years behind the wheel. He'd make a pot on Saturday mornings and offer it to anyone who came to the taxi stand — paying passengers, broke travelers, neighbours who just needed a warm bowl.

"The road will wear you down. But a bowl of souse and an open Bible? That's enough for any mile."
On Johnny Bread: Don't buy it. Make it. The whole point of Johnny Bread is that it's made with your hands, not your wallet. Tear it apart and dip it into the broth. That's the tradition.
The Authors

Cleveland Maxwell "Max" Rolle

Co-Author · Taxi Driver · Tour Operator · Bimini, Bahamas

Max grew up on the island of Bimini, where the water is close and the road is short but the stories run deep. He spent over 25 years ferrying tourists and locals alike through those miles — until a single encounter in a prison cell changed everything. He met God in the dark, and he has been driving toward the light ever since.

"Every passenger is a gift. Every mile is a ministry. I just learned to show up and let God drive the rest."

Karenda Samantha Rolle

Co-Author · Writer · Max's Wife

Karenda sat across the table from Max and wrote down what she heard — not just the stories, but the grace buried inside them. She gave voice and shape to decades of ordinary road moments that turned out to be anything but. This book is her husband's testimony, told through her pen, offered to anyone willing to read.
